The hundredth season, which raised Terrebonne and Kennebecasis to Earl and Rosedale to Viscount and created Beauvais of Charles-LeMoyne outright, turned out to be the opening of the peerage's noisiest half-century rather than the settled close of its first. Thirty-eight elevations fell inside these fifty-one seasons; ninety-one disputes were pressed where the whole previous run had produced thirteen; and the map, three-fifths claimed at the hundredth, passed four-fifths in the hundred and thirtieth and stood at five-sixths by the hundred and fiftieth. What changed was not temper but room. By the hundred and fiftieth, forty-five of the fifty-eight standing houses were enclosed — hemmed in on every side by another house's ground — and a house with nowhere to grow presses on its neighbours instead.

The first sign was that the great houses began to come apart under their own weight. Bellechasse split in the hundred and fifth, its holder's death throwing off Baron Benjamin of La Pointe-de-l'Île; LaSalle split in the hundred and seventh, Emerson in the hundred and fifteenth, Steveston in the hundred and sixteenth, Glengarry in the hundred and twenty-fourth. Seven cadet houses were founded by partition in these seasons against one in all the seasons before, and in the hundred and forty-eighth Hespeler — itself a cadet struck off Perth — was large enough to throw off a cadet of its own at Hamilton.

Beside them the engine went on creating houses outright: Smoke of Barrie in the hundred and first, a Haudenosaunee house; Chapais de Saint-Hyacinthe; Dickson of Moncton; Uniacke of Bowmanville; Jarvis of Spadina; Dyck of Calgary McKnight; Shawanaga of Waterloo; Cogswell of Argentia in Newfoundland. Not all of them held. Fairbanks of Edmonton Griesbach, which had gathered five Alberta ridings inside forty seasons, failed in the hundred and eighth for want of a successor and all of it went back to the Crown — the second house the game has lost.

The third was lost differently, and it was the largest thing that happened in these fifty seasons. In the hundredth season Preston had married its heir to Terrebonne. In the hundred and twelfth Alexander Forbes succeeded cleanly to the Marquisate; in the hundred and sixteenth he died leaving no one, and because the kin bond of the hundredth season stood between them, Preston did not escheat but was absorbed entire by Terrebonne. Eight of the seventeen ridings Terrebonne holds today were Preston's. The first house ever raised above Earl ended as a clause in the inheritance of the house it had married into.

Quarrelling became ordinary. A hundred and nine grievances opened between the hundredth season and the hundred and fiftieth, against twenty-two in the fifty-nine seasons before. Four came of letters that gave offence; the record names what the other hundred and five were over: thirty-two over the water rights of a shared river, twenty-four over a boundary the two houses read differently, twenty-one over the patronage of a shared riding's offices, fifteen over a slighted marriage, thirteen over a church and school dispute in the border townships. Ninety-one disputes were pressed over them. Forty-four were won and forty-seven rebuffed, and — this is the whole of it — a won dispute takes no ground. It costs the loser standing and settles the quarrel, and the peerage of these seasons chose it over anything sharper more than ninety times.

Once only did it harden. In the hundred and fifteenth Steinbach carried a dispute against Emerson and the grievance did not resolve but set, the single instance in a hundred and fifty seasons of a quarrel passing into open hostility. Eight seasons later Emerson challenged Steinbach and failed. That is the only challenge in the entire record, and it took nothing. In a hundred and fifty seasons not one riding anywhere on the map has been carried by force.

What ground did move, moved by failure or by agreement. Twenty-five quarrels were settled by a ceded riding and twenty-two by reconciliation. Ten holders died with no heir named, and six of those disorderly successions cost the house a riding outright: Perth gave up Hamilton West—Ancaster—Dundas in the hundred and twenty-fifth, Emerson gave up Portage—Lisgar in the hundred and twenty-seventh having already sold Brandon—Souris under pressure that same season, Beloeil gave up Mount Royal in the hundred and thirtieth, Bellechasse gave up Chicoutimi—Le Fjord in the hundred and thirty-seventh, and Charlevoix gave up Nunavut in the hundred and forty-seventh, which ended the peerage's reach into that territory altogether.

Some ground would not settle anywhere. Burlington was ceded away three separate times by three different houses — Oakville in the hundred and sixteenth, Milton in the hundred and twenty-fifth, Barrie in the hundred and forty-fourth. Central Newfoundland was taken by Charlevoix in the hundred and second, ceded by it in the hundred and twenty-fifth and ceded again by Bellechasse in the hundred and thirtieth. Chicoutimi—Le Fjord was let go by two houses in turn, and in the hundred and forty-first Trudel de Chicoutimi was created there on ground nobody had been able to keep.

The hundred and fortieth season saw two purchases in one stroke — Émard bought Laurier—Sainte-Marie from Beloeil and Bowmanville bought Hastings—Lennox and Addington—Tyendinaga from Belleville, both at forty — the second and third ridings ever to pass from one house to another for a price, after Bellechasse's single purchase in the sixty-eighth.

Meanwhile the ladder ran faster than it ever had. Chatham was raised to Marquis in the hundred and fifteenth, Rimouski in the hundred and sixteenth, Rosedale in the hundred and twenty-second, Beloeil in the hundred and twenty-third, Kennebecasis in the hundred and thirty-seventh, Wawa in the hundred and forty-fourth, Bellechasse in the hundred and forty-fifth, Brampton in the hundred and forty-ninth and Charles-LeMoyne in the hundred and fiftieth. Nine houses stand at Marquis and none above; the peerage has never made a Duke. Nor has the ladder lifted many: of the fifty-eight houses standing, twenty-two are still barons.

At the close of the hundred and fiftieth season fifty-eight houses held two hundred and ninety-one of the three hundred and forty-three ridings, and fifty-two were left unclaimed. Three houses have been lost in a hundred and fifty seasons, and all three to a failure of inheritance rather than to a rival. The three era-cohorts have drawn further apart than ever — the confederation ledger has taken two hundred and twenty-three entries and stands at +83 conservative, the dominion ninety-six and stands at +10, the late thirty-eight and stands at +2 — and yet the clocks themselves have converged, because clocks sync where houses meet and these houses meet constantly. In the hundred and fiftieth season Bellechasse, Terrebonne, Lac-Saint-Louis, Cumberland, North Battleford, Regina and Mistassini all met Newfoundland's entry into Confederation in the same personal 1949, having arrived at that year not together but by way of each other.
