Season 100

Read the narrative for seasons 42–100 chronicle

The border the last chronicle left standing at fifty-one points caught in the forty-fourth season. Bellechasse and Charlevoix fell out over the water rights of a shared river, and it proved to be the opening of the longest quarrel in the record rather than the end of a long wait. In the same seasons the peerage began to widen past the founding communities: Bird of Winnipeg in the forty-fourth and Cardinal of Steinbach in the forty-sixth, both Cree and Saulteaux houses seated in the same city; Rouleau de LaSalle in the forty-seventh; Hendrie of Brampton in the forty-ninth. Rochon wrote a letter in the forty-seventh that gave offence to Kennebecasis, and Kennebecasis made peace over it seven seasons later.

The fiftieth season took four holders in one stroke: Glengarry, Perth, Terrebonne and Kennebecasis all buried theirs, and all four successions were clean. Not a riding moved between houses that season and not a quarrel opened in it, which is the shape the whole run would keep: of the fifty successions between the forty-second season and the hundredth, forty-three passed without disorder, six in disorder and one not at all. Bhullar of Nanaimo was created in the fifty-second, a Punjabi Sikh house on Vancouver Island, and in that same season Bellechasse carried a dispute against Charlevoix and won it — one of only two disputes pressed to a conclusion in fifty-nine seasons.

Cousineau de Wiarton was created in the fifty-third and failed in the sixtieth, its two ridings returning to the Crown for want of a successor: the first house the game has lost. Around it the map went on filling. Pegahmagabow of Wawa came in the fifty-fourth, an Anishinaabe house at Sault Ste. Marie—Algoma; Byrne of Cooksville in the fifty-sixth and Meagher of Windsor in the fifty-seventh; Marchand de Lac-Saint-Louis in the fifty-eighth. Beloeil, which had already once passed in disorder, did so again in the fifty-sixth and gave up Hochelaga—Rosemont-Est for it — the only riding in the hundred seasons lost to a failure of inheritance, where every other that changed hands was ceded, sold or bought.

Then the ladder began to move. In the sixty-first season Hays of Beloeil was raised to Earl, the first elevation the peerage had ever granted — twenty-one seasons after Bellechasse had petitioned for one and been refused. Eleven more followed before the hundredth. The most consequential was the sixty-eighth, when Elliott of Glengarry was raised to Viscount and closed the objective it had drawn in the first season of the game: sixty-seven seasons and three holders between the ambition and the grant.

The sixty-eighth season also saw the one transaction in the whole record that was neither taken nor given. Bellechasse bought Beauport—Limoilou from Terrebonne for forty — the only riding ever to pass directly from one house to another for a price. It is of a piece with how that house grew. Bellechasse pressed only two successful expansions in fifty-nine seasons, and yet ended with more ridings than any other: four of its ten came to it without a roll — three ceded out of quarrels it had won and one bought. It was challenged five times, by Charlevoix four times and by Rimouski once, and rebuffed every one of them.

That pattern held everywhere. Between the forty-second season and the hundredth, eleven claims were pressed by arms and eleven were rebuffed; not one riding in fifty-nine seasons was carried by challenge. What quarrels there were ended in three ways instead. Nine were reconciled, five were settled by a ceded riding, and the rest simply lapsed, the two houses ceasing to think the matter worth the trouble. Against twenty-two grievances the record sets two hundred and ten correspondences, thirty-eight compacts and twenty-one marriages: the peerage of these seasons quarrelled at the margins and bound itself at the centre.

The seventy-first season opened the west properly, with Fairbanks of Edmonton Griesbach — which would take five of the six Alberta ridings any house holds — in the middle of the run of foundings that carried Gillies of Woburn, Lucas of Oakville, Dawes of Bourassa and Turgeon de St.-Jerome onto the map. Preston and Belfast had fallen out in the seventieth over water rights, the beginning of a courteous, twenty-five-season exchange in which Belfast pressed a claim against Preston twice and was rebuffed twice, and each house in the end ceded the other a riding: Preston gave up Dartmouth—Cole Harbour in the eighty-second, Belfast gave up Miramichi—Grand Lake in the ninety-fifth, and by the hundredth a grievance between them stood open again. In the seventy-third Emerson carried the second and last successful dispute, against Steinbach, and Preston passed in disorder.

In the seventy-sixth Wallace of Perth did what no house had done before: it grew large enough that its holder's death split it. Baron Wallace of Hespeler was founded by partition out of Perth in the same season Sarah Wallace succeeded to the parent house — a cadet line with a full share of ground and almost none of the standing, which is still true of it. Steinbach settled its own quarrel with Winnipeg by ceding St. Boniface—St. Vital in the seventy-seventh, and Emerson made peace with Winnipeg two seasons after that.

One house ran clean away from the rest. Terrebonne, under a single long-lived holder, drove its personal clock further than any line in the game: it met the Long Contraction in its 1930, the founding of the CCF in 1932, the opening of the Bank of Canada in 1935, the Second World War in 1939, the internment of Japanese Canadians in 1942 and the CCF's Saskatchewan government in 1944, and it took Bellechasse—Les Etchemins—Lévis in the middle of all that. Bellechasse reached 1932 and Perth 1925; no other house's furthest year passed 1920. The three era-cohorts consequently ran side by side and at very different depths — the confederation ledger has taken one hundred and forty-four entries and stands at +54 conservative, the dominion ledger twenty-nine and stands at +7, and the late ledger six and stands level.

The last dozen seasons were mostly the ladder and the ledger. Charlevoix and Chatham were both raised to Earl in the eighty-sixth; Simcoe passed in disorder in the eighty-seventh; Trois-Rivières and Rimouski each ceded a riding to Bellechasse in the eighty-eighth and eighty-ninth. In the ninety-sixth Forbes of Preston was raised to Marquis, the first house ever to stand above Earl. The hundredth season raised three more — Terrebonne and Kennebecasis to Earl, Rosedale to Viscount — married Preston to Terrebonne, which pulled Terrebonne's runaway clock back to Preston's 1894, and created Beauvais of Charles-LeMoyne at the rank of Earl outright.

At the close of the hundredth season thirty-eight houses stood, one having failed, and they held two hundred and seven of three hundred and forty-three ridings — in every province and in all three territories, with Nanaimo holding the Yukon and the Northwest Territories and Charlevoix holding Nunavut and Labrador from the far side of the country. The ground and the standing have come apart: Hespeler holds eight ridings on twelve points of influence and Cumberland seven on thirteen, while Steinbach sits at the ceiling of influence on two, and the three largest treasuries in the peerage belong to Émard, Aurora and Trois-Rivières, which hold one riding, two and one. Three grievances remain open — Bellechasse against Charlevoix, Bellechasse against Trois-Rivières, and Preston against Belfast — and forty-two of the sixty-five borders the record tracks are carrying friction that has not yet caught.

Season 41

Read the narrative for seasons 1–41 chronicle

Baron Elliott of Glengarry was created in the first season and seated at Kingston and the Islands — an Irish Protestant house of the Orange interest, mixed in temper, with far more cohesion than capital and almost no ambition to spend. It held the field alone for three seasons. Thomas Elliott rested, then cultivated influence, and in his personal 1869 met the Red River Resistance and exploited it; in 1870 he set his face against the Manitoba Act, and in 1871 turned British Columbia's entry and the signing of Treaty 1 to profit. The company he would keep arrived while he was doing it: Viscount Wallace of Perth in the fourth season, a Toronto commercial house tagged Outside and seated at Niagara West, and in the fifth Baron Stefaniuk of Emerson at Provencher — Ukrainian, conservative, and the most ambitious thing yet made.

Wallace was the first to take ground. In the sixth season it took Haldimand—Norfolk while Elliott and Stefaniuk were naming heirs, and in the seventh it wrote the first letter in the record, to Emerson. Elliott reached four times for a neighbouring riding in those early seasons and was refused four times. Viscount Benjamin of Bellechasse followed in the eighth, a Jewish Montréal house of conservative temper seated at Laurentides—Labelle, and then the pace of creation quickened: Mercier de Rimouski in the ninth, Bouchard de Terrebonne in the tenth, Hays of Beloeil in the eleventh. Four of the first seven houses were Québec houses, and Bellechasse would in time share a land border with three of them.

The eleventh season set the pattern that governed everything after. Stefaniuk, whose founding objective was a compact, married Kost Stefaniuk to George Wallace and bound Emerson to Perth; Mercier took Repentigny; Wallace's clock ran on to 1874. Kinship, not arms, was how these houses grew into one another. In the twelfth Thomas Elliott died and Mary Elliott succeeded cleanly, the first of ten successions in forty-one seasons and the first of nine that gave no trouble at all. Baron McLellan of Belfast was created in the thirteenth on Prince Edward Island and Baron Cornwall of Steveston in the fourteenth at Victoria, putting a house on each far coast within a single season of each other.

Elliott's long drought broke in the fourteenth at Lanark—Frontenac and again in the fifteenth at Carleton, and Benjamin endowed an institution — the first of five endowments, and the only kind of act in the whole record made for nothing but its own sake. Baron Langelier de Charlevoix came in the seventeenth and Viscount Meagher of Chatham in the eighteenth, the latter created with more influence than any house before it and seated far north at Kapuskasing—Timmins—Mushkegowuk. In that same season Mercier took Pierre-Boucher—Les Patriotes—Verchères and Hays took Outremont, and the crowding of the St. Lawrence began in earnest.

The twentieth season was the hinge. Norah Elliott married Emanuel Benjamin, binding Glengarry to Bellechasse across the Ottawa; Wallace endowed; Baron Hoover of Simcoe was created at Ottawa South. Twelve houses now stood, and the engine created no more for fourteen seasons. What followed was consolidation rather than settlement: Wallace worked steadily outward through the Niagara peninsula, taking Niagara Falls—Niagara-on-the-Lake in the twenty-first and Flamborough—Glanbrook—Brant North in the twenty-third; Lomer Bouchard succeeded in the twenty-first and Hyacinthe Mercier in the twenty-fifth; Hoover endowed in the twenty-fourth and Langelier in the twenty-seventh. McLellan wrote to Mercier in three consecutive seasons and would write, and wait, eleven seasons more before the two houses agreed to anything.

Underneath the correspondence, pressure was accumulating where nobody was looking. The friction between Bellechasse and Rimouski — neighbours in Québec, one conservative and one progressive — came to the breaking point in the twenty-first season and again in the twenty-seventh, and each time failed to catch. The societal ledger meanwhile ran one way and did not stop. Every event of these forty-one seasons belonged to the confederation cohort, and that cohort's climate, level at the outset, was carried steadily conservative across thirty-nine recorded entries by the Long Depression, the National Policy, the Indian Act, the Manitoba Schools Question and the Jesuits' Estates Act Controversy, with the Pacific Scandal, the Montréal Smallpox Epidemic and Laurier's election too few and too light between them to answer it.

The houses answered the drift by drawing together. Stefaniuk carried the plains west into Saskatchewan at Yorkton—Melville in the twenty-sixth; Kost Stefaniuk succeeded in the twenty-ninth and Anna Hoover in the thirtieth. In the thirtieth a letter from Bellechasse gave offence to Belfast — the only piece of correspondence in the whole record to do harm — and Benjamin spent the thirty-third season making peace over it. That thirty-third was the most cooperative season of the game: Bouchard entered into a compact with McLellan and Meagher with Hays, both in the same breath as the reconciliation, and two seasons earlier Elliott and Hays had made the first compact of all. Mercier and McLellan followed in the thirty-fifth.

Creation resumed in the thirty-fourth with Earl Forbes of Preston at Acadie—Annapolis — the only house yet raised above viscount, and the most influential any house had been at its founding. Baron Rochon de Rosedale followed in the thirty-sixth at Don Valley West, Franco-Ontarian and conservative, and took Beaches—East York two seasons later. The thirty-sixth and thirty-seventh were hard on Beloeil: Lazarus Hays succeeded his father, then died in the thirty-seventh with no heir named, and the house passed in disorder to Sarah Hays. Its clock was set back to 1867, and its cohesion, which at its founding had been among the highest recorded, now stands lower than that of any house created before the thirty-fourth season.

The grievance that had been building for eighteen seasons finally caught in the thirty-ninth, and the record names what it was over: a boundary the two houses read differently. Benjamin and Mercier fell out. They settled it in the fortieth without a blow — Mercier gave up Longueuil—Saint-Hubert to Bellechasse by cession, and the quarrel was marked resolved. In the same season Benjamin petitioned for elevation and was refused; Blanche Bouchard married Elspeth McLellan; Wallace took Perth—Wellington and McLellan Cumberland—Colchester; Baron Patterson of Kennebecasis was created at Acadie—Bathurst. The forty-first brought Donald Forbes and Clémence Langelier together, Frances Cornwall to Steveston, and Viscount Cameron of Cornwall to Richmond Hill South.

At the close of the forty-first season sixteen houses held fifty-two of three hundred and forty-three ridings. Not one house has been removed, no dispute has been laid and no challenge fought: both quarrels the record admits were settled, one by reconciliation and one by ceded ground. Wallace holds eight ridings; Meagher, which has more influence than any house alive, holds one and has not once in twenty-three seasons reached for a second. The confederation climate stands at +15 conservative, and the dominion and late cohorts have not been touched at all. Wallace's clock reads 1904 and Cameron's 1867. And the border between Bellechasse and Charlevoix, which has never yet caught, now carries fifty-one points of friction against a threshold of sixty.
